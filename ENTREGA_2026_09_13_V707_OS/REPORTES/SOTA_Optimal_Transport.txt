## 1. HISTORICAL TRAJECTORY & ML SURVEY

The mathematics of optimal transport was initiated by Gaspard Monge (1781) in *Mémoire sur la théorie des déblais et des remblais*, formulating the problem of moving a mass of soil from an initial configuration to a target configuration with minimal mechanical work. Monge's formulation was inherently non-linear and constrained: it demanded a point-to-point transport map $T: X \to Y$, frequently resulting in non-existence when mass splitting was required.

A century and a half later, Leonid Kantorovich (1942) formulated the modern linear relaxation, replacing transport maps with joint probability measures (transport plans) $\gamma \in \mathcal{P}(X \times Y)$ whose marginals match the prescribed source and target distributions. Kantorovich established the duality theory that bears his name, laying the foundation for modern linear programming and infinite-dimensional convex analysis (Kantorovich, 1942; Nobel Prize in Economics, 1975).

The geometric and analytical foundations were deepened through the late 20th century:
- **Yann Brenier (1991):** Proved the polar factorization theorem on $\mathbb{R}^D$, showing that for quadratic Euclidean cost $c(x, y) = \|x - y\|_2^2$, if the source measure $\mu$ is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, the optimal Kantorovich transport plan is unique and supported on the graph of a Monge map $T = \nabla \phi$, where $\phi: \mathbb{R}^D \to \mathbb{R}$ is a strictly convex potential function.
- **Robert McCann (1997):** Introduced displacement interpolation, demonstrating that the space of probability measures endowed with the $W_2$ metric forms a geodesic metric space where entropy functionals exhibit displacement convexity, establishing deep bridges to interacting gas dynamics and partial differential equations.
- **Jean-David Benamou & Yann Brenier (2000):** Developed the dynamic Eulerian formulation of OT, recasting $W_2$ as the geodesic distance in an infinite-dimensional Riemannian manifold of densities governed by the continuity equation (fluid dynamics formulation).
- **Cédric Villani (2003, 2008):** Synthesized the complete analytical, geometric, and probabilistic theory in *Topics in Optimal Transportation* (2003) and *Optimal Transport: Old and New* (2008), formalizing Otto calculus and Ricci curvature lower bounds via optimal transport (Lott-Sturm-Villani theory).

### 1.1 The Modern Computational Era in Machine Learning

Despite its geometric elegance, optimal transport remained largely absent from large-scale machine learning for decades due to prohibitive computational costs: exact discrete OT via interior point or network simplex solvers requires $\mathcal{O}(N^3 \log N)$ operations for $N$ support points.

The computational breakthrough came with **Marco Cuturi (2013)** in *Sinkhorn Distances: Lightspeed Computation of Optimal Transport*. By augmenting the Kantorovich objective with an entropic regularization term $\varepsilon H(\gamma)$, Cuturi transformed the constrained linear program into a strictly convex optimization problem solvable via the classical **Sinkhorn-Knopp matrix-scaling algorithm** (Sinkhorn, 1964). This reduced the complexity to $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ per iteration, executable entirely through batched matrix multiplications on massively parallel GPU architectures.

Subsequent pivotal breakthroughs expanded this framework:
- **Wasserstein Generative Adversarial Networks (WGAN; Arjovsky, Chintala, & Bottou, 2017):** Exploited the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality for the $W_1$ metric:
  $$W_1(\mu, \nu) = \sup_{\|f\|_{\text{Lip}} \le 1} \left( \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mu}[f(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \nu}[f(y)] \right)$$
  By parameterizing $f$ as a deep neural network constrained to be 1-Lipschitz (via weight clipping or gradient penalty), Arjovsky et al. circumvented mode collapse and vanishing gradients inherent in Jensen-Shannon divergence, establishing Wasserstein metrics as stable training objectives for probability distributions on low-dimensional submanifolds embedded in high dimensions.
- **Sinkhorn Divergences (Genevay, Peyré, & Cuturi, 2018):** Addressed the persistent entropic bias of regularized transport. Unregularized OT satisfies $W(\mu, \mu) = 0$, but entropic OT yields $W_{c, \varepsilon}(\mu, \mu) > 0$. Genevay et al. proposed the debiased Sinkhorn divergence:
  $$S_\varepsilon(\mu, \nu) = W_{c, \varepsilon}(\mu, \nu) - \frac{1}{2} W_{c, \varepsilon}(\mu, \mu) - \frac{1}{2} W_{c, \varepsilon}(\nu, \nu)$$
  proving that $S_\varepsilon(\mu, \nu) \ge 0$ with equality if and only if $\mu = \nu$, interpolating smoothly between unregularized OT ($\varepsilon \to 0$) and Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) with a negative distance kernel ($\varepsilon \to \infty$).
- **Foundations and Computational Landscape (Peyré & Cuturi, 2019):** In *Computational Optimal Transport*, Gabriel Peyré and Marco Cuturi codified the theoretical, numerical, and statistical foundations of modern OT, spanning discrete scaling algorithms, stochastic approximations, gradient flows, and metric-measure space alignments.

## 2. MATHEMATICAL FOUNDATIONS OF OPTIMAL TRANSPORT

### 2.1 The Monge Problem

Let $(X, \mu)$ and $(Y, \nu)$ be two Polish probability spaces with $\mu \in \mathcal{P}(X)$ and $\nu \in \mathcal{P}(Y)$. Let $c: X \times Y \to [0, +\infty]$ be a Borel-measurable cost function representing the unit work required to transport mass from $x \in X$ to $y \in Y$.

A measurable map $T: X \to Y$ is called a **transport map** from $\mu$ to $\nu$ if it satisfies the pushforward condition:
$$T_\sharp \mu = \nu \iff \nu(B) = \mu(T^{-1}(B)) \quad \forall \text{ Borel } B \subseteq Y$$
Equivalently, for every bounded continuous test function $h \in C_b(Y)$:
$$\int_Y h(y) \, d\nu(y) = \int_X h(T(x)) \, d\mu(x)$$

The **Monge Problem** seeks to find a transport map $T$ that minimizes the total transportation cost:
$$\inf_{T: T_\sharp \mu = \nu} \mathcal{M}(T) := \int_X c(x, T(x)) \, d\mu(x) \tag{1}$$

#### Structural Obstructions of the Monge Formulation:
1. **Mass Splitting:** If $\mu = \delta_{x_0}$ (a single Dirac delta) and $\nu = \frac{1}{2}\delta_{y_1} + \frac{1}{2}\delta_{y_2}$ with $y_1 \neq y_2$, no deterministic map $T$ can split the unit mass at $x_0$ into two locations. Hence, the feasible set $\{T : T_\sharp \mu = \nu\}$ is empty.
2. **Non-Convexity:** The pushforward constraint $T_\sharp \mu = \nu$ is highly non-convex in $T$. Even when solutions exist, finding the infimum directly is an intractable infinite-dimensional non-convex variational problem.

---

### 2.2 The Kantorovich Relaxation

Kantorovich (1942) relaxed the deterministic mapping $T(x)$ to a joint probability measure $\gamma \in \mathcal{P}(X \times Y)$, termed a **transport plan**. The value $d\gamma(x, y)$ represents the infinitesimal quantity of mass transferred from $x$ to $y$.

Let $\pi_X: X \times Y \to X$ and $\pi_Y: X \times Y \to Y$ be the canonical projection operators $\pi_X(x, y) = x$ and $\pi_Y(x, y) = y$. The set of admissible transport plans with marginals $\mu$ and $\nu$ is defined as:
$$\Pi(\mu, \nu) = \left\{ \gamma \in \mathcal{P}(X \times Y) : (\pi_X)_\sharp \gamma = \mu, \, (\pi_Y)_\sharp \gamma = \nu \right\} \tag{2}$$
Explicitly, for all Borel subsets $A \subseteq X$ and $B \subseteq Y$:
$$\gamma(A \times Y) = \mu(A) \quad \text{and} \quad \gamma(X \times B) = \nu(B)$$

The **Kantorovich Problem** is formulated as the infinite-dimensional linear program:
$$\mathcal{K}_c(\mu, \nu) = \inf_{\gamma \in \Pi(\mu, \nu)} \int_{X \times Y} c(x, y) \, d\gamma(x, y) \tag{3}$$

#### Fundamental Existence Theorem:
If $X$ and $Y$ are Polish spaces, $c: X \times Y \to [0, +\infty]$ is lower semicontinuous, and there exist measurable functions $a \in L^1(\mu), b \in L^1(\nu)$ such that $c(x, y) \ge a(x) + b(y)$, then:
1. The admissible set $\Pi(\mu, \nu)$ is non-empty, convex, and weakly compact in $\mathcal{P}(X \times Y)$ (by Prokhorov's theorem).
2. The infimum in Equation (3) is attained by an optimal transport plan $\gamma^* \in \Pi(\mu, \nu)$.

### 2.3 Dual Formulation & Kantorovich-Rubinstein Duality

By applying the Fenchel-Rockafellar duality theorem to the constrained convex optimization problem (3), we obtain the dual formulation:
$$\mathcal{K}_c(\mu, \nu) = \sup_{(u, v) \in \Phi_c} \left\{ \int_X u(x) \, d\mu(x) + \int_Y v(y) \, d\nu(y) \right\} \tag{4}$$
where the dual constraint set $\Phi_c$ is defined by:
$$\Phi_c = \left\{ (u, v) \in L^1(\mu) \times L^1(\nu) : u(x) + v(y) \le c(x, y) \quad \forall (x, y) \in X \times Y \right\} \tag{5}$$

For any function $u: X \to \mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty\}$, its **$c$-transform** $u^c: Y \to \mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty\}$ is defined as:
$$u^c(y) = \inf_{x \in X} \left\{ c(x, y) - u(x) \right\}$$
A function $u$ is said to be **$c$-concave** if $u = (u^c)^{\bar{c}}$, where $\bar{c}(y, x) = c(x, y)$. Under mild regularity conditions, the dual problem reduces to optimizing over a single $c$-concave potential:
$$\mathcal{K}_c(\mu, \nu) = \sup_{u \in C_c(X)} \left\{ \int_X u(x) \, d\mu(x) + \int_Y u^c(y) \, d\nu(y) \right\} \tag{6}$$

#### The Kantorovich-Rubinstein Theorem ($p=1$):
When $X = Y = (M, d)$ is a metric space and the cost is the metric itself, $c(x, y) = d(x, y)$, a function $u$ is $c$-concave if and only if it is 1-Lipschitz with respect to $d$:
$$|u(x) - u(y)| \le d(x, y) \quad \forall x, y \in M$$
In this setting, $u^c(y) = -u(y)$, yielding the **Kantorovich-Rubinstein Duality**:
$$W_1(\mu, \nu) = \sup_{\|f\|_{\text{Lip}} \le 1} \left\{ \int_M f(x) \, d\mu(x) - \int_M f(y) \, d\nu(y) \right\} \tag{7}$$
This dual form eliminates the quadratic coupling over $X \times Y$, replacing it with separate expectations over $\mu$ and $\nu$.

### 2.4 Wasserstein-$p$ Metrics & Riemannian Geometry of $\mathcal{P}_2(M)$

Let $(M, d)$ be a complete, separable metric space. For $p \in [1, \infty)$, the **Wasserstein-$p$ space** $\mathcal{P}_p(M)$ is the set of probability measures on $M$ with finite $p$-th moments:
$$\mathcal{P}_p(M) = \left\{ \mu \in \mathcal{P}(M) : \int_M d(x_0, x)^p \, d\mu(x) < \infty \text{ for some (and hence all) } x_0 \in M \right\}$$

The **Wasserstein-$p$ distance** between $\mu, \nu \in \mathcal{P}_p(M)$ is:
$$W_p(\mu, \nu) = \left( \inf_{\gamma \in \Pi(\mu, \nu)} \int_{M \times M} d(x, y)^p \, d\gamma(x, y) \right)^{1/p} \tag{8}$$
For $p = \infty$:
$$W_\infty(\mu, \nu) = \inf_{\gamma \in \Pi(\mu, \nu)} \operatorname{ess\,sup}_{(x, y) \sim \gamma} d(x, y)$$

#### Metric Properties:
- $W_p$ satisfies the metric axioms: non-negativity, identity of indiscernibles ($W_p(\mu, \nu) = 0 \iff \mu = \nu$), symmetry, and the triangle inequality (Minkowski's inequality on $L^p(\gamma)$).
- **Topology:** $W_p$ metrizes the topology of weak convergence plus convergence of $p$-th order moments. That is, $W_p(\mu_k, \mu) \to 0$ if and only if $\mu_k \rightharpoonup \mu$ weakly and $\int d(x_0, x)^p d\mu_k(x) \to \int d(x_0, x)^p d\mu(x)$.

#### Otto Calculus and the Benamou-Brenier Formulation:
On Euclidean space $M = \mathbb{R}^D$ with $p=2$, the space $(\mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R}^D), W_2)$ possesses a formal infinite-dimensional Riemannian manifold structure (Otto, 2001). The tangent space at an absolutely continuous measure $\mu$ is defined as:
$$T_\mu \mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R}^D) \cong \overline{\left\{ \nabla \phi : \phi \in C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R}^D) \right\}}^{L^2(\mu)}$$
For two tangent vectors $\nabla \phi_1, \nabla \phi_2 \in T_\mu \mathcal{P}_2$, the Riemannian metric tensor is:
$$\langle \nabla \phi_1, \nabla \phi_2 \rangle_\mu = \int_{\mathbb{R}^D} \langle \nabla \phi_1(x), \nabla \phi_2(x) \rangle \, d\mu(x)$$

The geodesic distance between $\mu_0$ and $\mu_1$ is given by the dynamic **Benamou-Brenier formula** (2000):
$$W_2^2(\mu_0, \mu_1) = \inf_{(\rho_t, v_t)} \left\{ \int_0^1 \int_{\mathbb{R}^D} \|v_t(x)\|_2^2 \, \rho_t(x) \, dx \, dt \right\} \tag{9}$$
subject to the continuity equation:
$$\partial_t \rho_t + \nabla \cdot (\rho_t v_t) = 0, \quad \rho_0 = \mu_0, \quad \rho_1 = \mu_1$$
Here, $\rho_t$ represents the time-evolving density and $v_t$ is the velocity vector field driving the transport.

### 2.5 Entropic Regularization & The Sinkhorn Algorithm

For discrete empirical measures with support sizes $n$ and $m$:
$$\mu = \sum_{i=1}^n a_i \delta_{x_i}, \quad \nu = \sum_{j=1}^m b_j \delta_{y_j}, \quad a \in \Delta^n, \, b \in \Delta^m$$
where $\Delta^n = \{ p \in \mathbb{R}_+^n : \sum_{i=1}^n p_i = 1 \}$. Let $C \in \mathbb{R}_+^{n \times m}$ be the cost matrix with entries $C_{ij} = c(x_i, y_j)$. The discrete transport polytope is:
$$U(a, b) = \left\{ P \in \mathbb{R}_+^{n \times m} : P \mathbf{1}_m = a, \, P^T \mathbf{1}_n = b \right\}$$
The classical unregularized Kantorovich problem is:
$$\min_{P \in U(a, b)} \langle P, C \rangle_F = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^m P_{ij} C_{ij}$$

#### Entropic Regularization (Cuturi, 2013):
Cuturi introduced an entropic penalty governed by regularization strength $\varepsilon > 0$:
$$W_{c, \varepsilon}(a, b) = \min_{P \in U(a, b)} \langle P, C \rangle_F - \varepsilon H(P) \tag{10}$$
where the discrete Shannon entropy is defined as:
$$H(P) = -\sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^m P_{ij} (\log P_{ij} - 1)$$
Because $-H(P)$ is 1-strongly convex with respect to the $\ell_1$-norm on the simplex, the objective in Equation (10) is strictly convex and has a unique global minimizer $P^*$.

#### Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) Conditions:
Forming the Lagrangian with dual multipliers $f \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $g \in \mathbb{R}^m$ for the marginal constraints:
$$\mathcal{L}(P, f, g) = \sum_{i,j} P_{ij} C_{ij} + \varepsilon \sum_{i,j} P_{ij}(\log P_{ij} - 1) - \langle f, P \mathbf{1}_m - a \rangle - \langle g, P^T \mathbf{1}_n - b \rangle$$
Taking the gradient with respect to $P_{ij}$ and setting it to zero:
$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial P_{ij}} = C_{ij} + \varepsilon \log P_{ij} - f_i - g_j = 0 \implies P_{ij}^* = \exp\left( \frac{f_i + g_j - C_{ij}}{\varepsilon} \right)$$
Factorizing the exponential gives:
$$P_{ij}^* = u_i K_{ij} v_j \iff P^* = \operatorname{diag}(u) K \operatorname{diag}(v) \tag{11}$$
where $K \in \mathbb{R}_+^{n \times m}$ is the **Gibbs kernel**:
$$K_{ij} = \exp\left( -\frac{C_{ij}}{\varepsilon} \right)$$
and the positive vectors $u \in \mathbb{R}_+^n, v \in \mathbb{R}_+^m$ are defined by $u_i = \exp(f_i / \varepsilon)$ and $v_j = \exp(g_j / \varepsilon)$.

#### The Sinkhorn-Knopp Iteration:
The marginal constraints $P \mathbf{1}_m = a$ and $P^T \mathbf{1}_n = b$ translate directly into coupled non-linear equations for $u$ and $v$:
$$\operatorname{diag}(u) K v = a \implies u \odot (K v) = a \implies u = \frac{a}{K v}$$
$$\operatorname{diag}(v) K^T u = b \implies v \odot (K^T u) = b \implies v = \frac{b}{K^T u}$$
where division and vector multiplication $\odot$ are element-wise.

The **Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm** alternates between these two updates:
$$u^{(t+1)} = \frac{a}{K v^{(t)}}, \quad v^{(t+1)} = \frac{b}{K^T u^{(t+1)}} \tag{12}$$

```
================================================================================
ALGORITHM 1: Sinkhorn-Knopp Matrix Scaling
================================================================================
Input: Cost matrix C in R_+^{n x m}, Marginals a in Delta^n, b in Delta^m,
       Regularization epsilon > 0, MaxIterations T, Tolerance tol
Output: Optimal transport plan P*, Entropic distance W_{c, epsilon}

1: K <- exp(-C / epsilon)          // Gibbs Kernel computation: O(n m)
2: u <- 1_n / n
3: v <- 1_m / m
4: for t = 1 to T do
5:     u_prev <- u
6:     u <- a / (K * v)            // Matrix-vector multiplication: O(n m)
7:     v <- b / (K^T * u)          // Matrix-vector multiplication: O(n m)
8:     if ||u - u_prev||_1 < tol then
9:         break
10:    end if
11: end for
12: P* <- diag(u) * K * diag(v)
13: W_{c, epsilon} <- sum_{i,j} P*_{ij} * C_{ij}
14: return P*, W_{c, epsilon}
================================================================================
```

#### Convergence Rate & Contractivity:
By Birkhoff's theorem and Hilbert's projective metric on the positive cone $\mathbb{R}_{++}^n$:
$$d_{\mathcal{H}}(x, y) = \ln \max_{i, j} \frac{x_i y_j}{x_j y_i}$$
The operator $\mathcal{T}(u) = \frac{a}{K (b / K^T u)}$ is a strict contraction under $d_{\mathcal{H}}$ with contraction ratio:
$$\kappa = \frac{\sqrt{\tau(K)} - 1}{\sqrt{\tau(K)} + 1} < 1, \quad \text{where } \tau(K) = \max_{i,j,k,l} \frac{K_{ik} K_{jl}}{K_{il} K_{jk}} = \exp\left( \frac{\operatorname{diam}(C)}{\varepsilon} \right)$$
Thus, Sinkhorn's algorithm exhibits **linear convergence**: $\|u^{(t)} - u^*\| \le \mathcal{O}(\kappa^t)$.

### 2.6 The Curse of Dimensionality in Empirical Optimal Transport

A critical theoretical barrier for applying standard optimal transport to high-dimensional AI is the **statistical rate of convergence of empirical measures**.

Let $\mu \in \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^D)$ and let $\hat{\mu}_N = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \delta_{X_i}$ be the empirical measure constructed from $N$ independent and identically distributed samples $X_i \sim \mu$. A fundamental question is: at what rate does $\mathbb{E}[W_p(\mu, \hat{\mu}_N)]$ converge to zero as $N \to \infty$?

#### The Dudley-Fournier-Guillin Theorem (Fournier & Guillin, 2015; Weed & Bach, 2019):
Assume $\mu$ satisfies a finite moment condition $\int_{\mathbb{R}^D} \|x\|_2^q d\mu(x) < \infty$ for $q > p$. Then, for dimension $D > 2p$:
$$\mathbb{E}[W_p^p(\mu, \hat{\mu}_N)] \asymp \mathcal{O}\left( N^{-p/D} \right) \implies \mathbb{E}[W_p(\mu, \hat{\mu}_N)] \asymp \mathcal{O}\left( N^{-1/D} \right) \tag{13}$$

#### Critical Implications for POLYDIM ($D \ge 10,000$):
In the POLYDIM operating regime where $D = 10,000$, the sample complexity required to estimate the true Wasserstein distance within an error threshold $\delta \in (0, 1)$ scales as:
$$N \ge \left( \frac{1}{\delta} \right)^D = \delta^{-10,000}$$
If $\delta = 0.5$, $N \ge 2^{10,000} \approx 10^{3,010}$, which vastly exceeds the number of elementary particles in the observable universe ($\sim 10^{80}$). 

Consequently:
> **Theorem 1 (Impossibility of High-Dimensional Empirical OT):** 
> *Full-dimensional empirical Wasserstein distance estimation without structural dimensional reduction or slicing is statistically meaningless at $D \ge 10,000$. Any empirical measure $\hat{\mu}_N$ with computationally feasible support size $N \le 10^6$ lies at maximal distance from its generating distribution $\mu$.*

This proves that POLYDIM cannot rely on naive empirical Wasserstein distances to compare high-dimensional continuous distributions without projected or sliced representations.

## 3. SLICED WASSERSTEIN DISTANCES IN HIGH DIMENSIONS

To circumvent the catastrophic curse of dimensionality $\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/D})$, modern computational optimal transport utilizes **projection-based metrics**, primarily the **Sliced Wasserstein Distance (SWD)** (Bonneel et al., 2015) and its generalizations.

### 3.1 Closed-Form Solution for One-Dimensional Optimal Transport

The core advantage of slicing stems from the fact that in dimension $D = 1$, optimal transport has an explicit, exact analytical solution requiring no optimization.

Let $\mu, \nu \in \mathcal{P}_p(\mathbb{R})$ with cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) $F_\mu(t) = \mu((-\infty, t])$ and $F_\nu(t) = \nu((-\infty, t])$. The quantile functions (generalized inverses) are:
$$F_\mu^{-1}(s) = \inf \{ t \in \mathbb{R} : F_\mu(t) \ge s \}, \quad s \in [0, 1]$$
Then, for any $p \ge 1$, the Wasserstein-$p$ distance is:
$$W_p^p(\mu, \nu) = \int_0^1 |F_\mu^{-1}(s) - F_\nu^{-1}(s)|^p \, ds \tag{14}$$

For discrete measures with $N$ uniform weights:
$$\mu = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \delta_{x_i}, \quad \nu = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^N \delta_{y_j}$$
let $x_{(1)} \le x_{(2)} \le \dots \le x_{(N)}$ and $y_{(1)} \le y_{(2)} \le \dots \le y_{(N)}$ denote the sorted order statistics. The optimal transport plan is strictly monotonic:
$$W_p^p(\mu, \nu) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N |x_{(i)} - y_{(i)}|^p \tag{15}$$
The computational complexity of 1D optimal transport is governed strictly by sorting: $\mathcal{O}(N \log N)$.

---

### 3.2 Sliced Wasserstein Distance Formulation

The Sliced Wasserstein Distance leverages the **Radon Transform** to project $D$-dimensional probability distributions onto one-dimensional lines.

For any unit vector $\theta \in \mathbb{S}^{D-1}$, define the linear projection operator $\pi_\theta: \mathbb{R}^D \to \mathbb{R}$ by:
$$\pi_\theta(x) = \langle x, \theta \rangle$$
The pushforward measure $\pi_\theta^\sharp \mu$ is a 1D probability measure on $\mathbb{R}$.

The **Sliced Wasserstein-$p$ Distance** (Bonneel et al., 2015) is defined as the integral of 1D Wasserstein distances over the uniform Haar measure $\sigma$ on $\mathbb{S}^{D-1}$:
$$\mathcal{SW}_p^p(\mu, \nu) = \int_{\mathbb{S}^{D-1}} W_p^p\left( \pi_\theta^\sharp \mu, \, \pi_\theta^\sharp \nu \right) \, d\sigma(\theta) \tag{16}$$

#### Metric Equivalence & Topological Properties:
$\mathcal{SW}_p$ is a true metric on $\mathcal{P}_p(\mathbb{R}^D)$ and is topologically equivalent to $W_p$:
$$c_1 \mathcal{SW}_p(\mu, \nu) \le W_p(\mu, \nu) \le c_2 \mathcal{SW}_p^{\alpha}(\mu, \nu)$$
for constants $c_1, c_2 > 0$ and $\alpha \in (0, 1]$ depending on the dimension and support diameter.

#### Monte Carlo Approximation:
In practice, the integral over the continuous sphere $\mathbb{S}^{D-1}$ is evaluated via Monte Carlo sampling with $L$ uniform random projections $\theta_1, \dots, \theta_L \sim \operatorname{Uniform}(\mathbb{S}^{D-1})$:
$$\widehat{\mathcal{SW}}_p^p(\mu, \nu) = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{\ell=1}^L W_p^p\left( \pi_{\theta_\ell}^\sharp \mu, \, \pi_{\theta_\ell}^\sharp \nu \right) \tag{17}$$

```
================================================================================
ALGORITHM 2: Monte Carlo Sliced Wasserstein-p Distance
================================================================================
Input: Samples X in R^{N x D} from mu, Samples Y in R^{N x D} from nu,
       Number of projections L, Order p >= 1
Output: Distance estimate SW_p

1: Generate L random vectors Theta ~ N(0, I_D) in R^{L x D}
2: Normalize rows: Theta_l <- Theta_l / ||Theta_l||_2   // Uniform on S^{D-1}
3: Project data:
     P_X <- X * Theta^T    // R^{N x L} matrix of 1D projections
     P_Y <- Y * Theta^T    // R^{N x L} matrix of 1D projections
4: Sort columns:
     S_X <- sort(P_X, axis=0)    // O(L * N log N)
     S_Y <- sort(P_Y, axis=0)    // O(L * N log N)
5: Compute L1/Lp residual:
     Diff <- |S_X - S_Y|^p
6: SW_p <- ( (1 / (L * N)) * sum(Diff) )^(1 / p)
7: return SW_p
================================================================================
```

#### Total Computational Complexity:
1. Matrix multiplication for projection: $\mathcal{O}(L \cdot N \cdot D)$.
2. Parallel 1D sorting across $L$ projections: $\mathcal{O}(L \cdot N \log N)$.
3. Averaging sorted residuals: $\mathcal{O}(L \cdot N)$.
$$\text{Total Complexity} = \mathcal{O}\left( L \cdot N \cdot D + L \cdot N \log N \right) \tag{18}$$

#### Statistical Sample Complexity (Breakdown of the Curse):
Unlike full-dimensional $W_p$, the empirical Sliced Wasserstein distance converges at the **parametric rate**:
$$\mathbb{E}\left[ \left| \mathcal{SW}_p(\mu, \hat{\mu}_N) \right| \right] \le \mathcal{O}\left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \right) \tag{19}$$
This rate is completely **independent of the ambient dimension $D$** (Nadjahi et al., 2019; Kolouri et al., 2019). Thus, at $D = 10,000$, $\mathcal{SW}_p$ remains statistically well-behaved with moderate particle sizes $N \sim 10^2 - 10^3$.

### 3.3 Advanced Variants: Max-SWD, GSW, and Spherical Sliced-Wasserstein

1. **Max-Sliced Wasserstein (Max-SWD; Deshpande et al., 2019):**
   Replaces the uniform expectation over $\mathbb{S}^{D-1}$ with the worst-case projection:
   $$\operatorname{MSW}_p(\mu, \nu) = \max_{\theta \in \mathbb{S}^{D-1}} W_p\left( \pi_\theta^\sharp \mu, \, \pi_\theta^\sharp \nu \right) \tag{20}$$
   Optimized via Riemannian gradient ascent on $\mathbb{S}^{D-1}$. MSW requires fewer projections ($L=1$ if globally optimized) but introduces non-convex optimization overhead.

2. **Generalized Sliced Wasserstein (GSW; Kolouri et al., 2019):**
   Replaces linear projections $\langle x, \theta \rangle$ with non-linear defining functions $g: \mathbb{R}^D \times \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ (such as circular, polynomial, or neural projections), formalizing slicing along non-linear manifolds:
   $$\mathcal{GSW}_p^p(\mu, \nu) = \int_\Omega W_p^p\left( g(\cdot, \theta)^\sharp \mu, \, g(\cdot, \theta)^\sharp \nu \right) \, d\sigma(\theta) \tag{21}$$

3. **Spherical Sliced-Wasserstein (SSW; Bonet et al., 2023):**
   Crucially relevant to POLYDIM, where all vectors inhabit $\mathbb{S}^{D-1}$. Projecting $\mathbb{S}^{D-1}$ linearly via $\langle x, \theta \rangle$ compresses geodesic spherical arcs into Euclidean chords on $[-1, 1]$. Bonet et al. formulate intrinsic slicing over elementary manifolds:
   - For points $x \in \mathbb{S}^{D-1}$, define the geodesic projection onto the circle $\mathbb{S}^1$ or interval $[0, \pi]$ via geodesic arc-length:
     $$g(x, \theta) = d_{\mathbb{S}^{D-1}}(x, \theta) = \arccos(\langle x, \theta \rangle) \in [0, \pi]$$
   - 1D transport is then performed along the one-dimensional geodesic distance distribution.

4. **Subspace Robust Wasserstein (SRW; Paty & Cuturi, 2019):**
   Instead of 1D projections, projects measures onto the optimal $k$-dimensional subspace $E \in \operatorname{Gr}(k, D)$ of the Grassmannian manifold:
   $$\mathcal{SRW}_k(\mu, \nu) = \max_{E \in \operatorname{Gr}(k, D)} W_2\left( P_E^\sharp \mu, \, P_E^\sharp \nu \right) \tag{22}$$
   SRW balances robustness against orthogonal noise with capturing multi-dimensional joint geometry, though computing the optimal subspace requires solving a max-min Stiefel optimization problem.

## 4. WASSERSTEIN BARYCENTERS AS CONSENSUS MECHANISMS

In a distributed multi-agent system, consensus corresponds to finding a representative state or distribution that minimizes aggregate divergence to all participating agents.

### 4.1 Formal Formulation (Agueh & Carlier, 2011)

Let $K$ agents broadcast probability measures $\mu_1, \dots, \mu_K \in \mathcal{P}_2(M)$, with agent reliability weights $\lambda = (\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_K) \in \Delta^K$ ($\sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k = 1, \lambda_k > 0$).

The **Wasserstein Barycenter** $\bar{\mu}$ is the Fréchet mean of $\{\mu_k\}_{k=1}^K$ on the Wasserstein space $(\mathcal{P}_2(M), W_2)$:
$$\bar{\mu} \in \arg\min_{\mu \in \mathcal{P}_2(M)} \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k W_2^2(\mu, \mu_k) \tag{23}$$

#### Existence and Uniqueness:
Agueh & Carlier (2011) proved that if at least one $\mu_k$ is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}^D$, the barycenter $\bar{\mu}$ is **unique** and absolutely continuous. Furthermore, the optimal transport maps $T_{\bar{\mu} \to \mu_k}$ satisfy the identity:
$$\sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k T_{\bar{\mu} \to \mu_k}(x) = x \quad \bar{\mu}\text{-almost everywhere} \tag{24}$$

---

### 4.2 Analytical Barycenters: The Gaussian and Dirac Regimes

#### 1. Gaussian Measures:
If every agent broadcasts a Gaussian distribution $\mu_k = \mathcal{N}(m_k, \Sigma_k)$ with mean $m_k \in \mathbb{R}^D$ and covariance $\Sigma_k \in \mathcal{S}_{++}^D$, the Wasserstein barycenter is strictly Gaussian:
$$\bar{\mu} = \mathcal{N}(\bar{m}, \bar{\Sigma})$$
where the barycentric mean is the exact Euclidean weighted centroid:
$$\bar{m} = \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k m_k \tag{25}$$
and the barycentric covariance $\bar{\Sigma}$ is the unique positive definite solution to the non-linear matrix fixed-point equation:
$$\bar{\Sigma} = \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k \left( \bar{\Sigma}^{1/2} \Sigma_k \bar{\Sigma}^{1/2} \right)^{1/2} \tag{26}$$

#### 2. Single Dirac Point Masses (Pure States):
Let each agent broadcast a deterministic state vector $x_k \in \mathbb{R}^D$, represented as a Dirac measure $\mu_k = \delta_{x_k}$.
Consider a candidate barycenter restricted to the space of Dirac measures $\mu = \delta_z$ for $z \in \mathbb{R}^D$. Then:
$$W_2^2(\delta_z, \delta_{x_k}) = \|z - x_k\|_2^2$$
The barycenter objective becomes:
$$\min_{z \in \mathbb{R}^D} \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k \|z - x_k\|_2^2 \tag{27}$$
Differentiating with respect to $z$:
$$2 \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k (z - x_k) = 0 \implies z^* = \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k x_k \tag{28}$$
The unconstrained Wasserstein barycenter of Dirac point masses in Euclidean space is **identically the Euclidean centroid**.

---

### 4.3 Free-Support vs. Fixed-Support Barycenters of Empirical Ensembles

When each agent broadcasts an empirical distribution of $N$ particles (e.g., token embeddings, hypotheses):
$$\mu_k = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \delta_{x_{k, i}}, \quad x_{k, i} \in \mathbb{R}^D$$

There are two primary paradigms to compute the consensus distribution $\bar{\mu} = \sum_{j=1}^S \beta_j \delta_{y_j}$:
1. **Fixed-Support (Grid-Based) Barycenters (Cuturi & Doucet, 2014):**
   The support locations $\{y_j\}_{j=1}^S$ are pre-selected on a fixed spatial grid, and optimization occurs solely over the weights $\beta \in \Delta^S$. Solved via iterative Bregman projections using the Sinkhorn kernel:
   $$\beta^{(t+1)} = \beta^{(t)} \odot \exp\left( \frac{1}{\varepsilon} \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k \log\left( K_k^T \frac{a_k}{K_k \beta^{(t)}} \right) \right)$$
   *Failure Mode at $D=10,000$:* A spatial grid on $\mathbb{R}^D$ requires $S \sim M^D$ points. In dimension $D=10,000$, fixed-support grids are physically impossible.
2. **Free-Support Barycenters:**
   Both the support locations $Y = (y_1, \dots, y_S) \in \mathbb{R}^{S \times D}$ and weights $\beta \in \Delta^S$ are optimized dynamically via alternating subgradient descent. At each iteration, $K$ independent optimal transport plans $P_k \in \mathbb{R}^{S \times N}$ are solved, and support positions are updated via:
   $$y_j^{(t+1)} = \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k \sum_{i=1}^N (P_k)_{ji} x_{k, i} \tag{29}$$
   Computational cost: $\mathcal{O}(I \cdot K \cdot S \cdot N \cdot D)$ where $I$ is the number of outer gradient iterations.

## 5. RIGOROUS CONNECTION TO POLYDIM & HIGH-DIMENSIONAL CONSENSUS

We now evaluate the core research question: **Can the Wasserstein barycenter replace the Euclidean Projected Centroid (EPC) for agent consensus in POLYDIM at $D \ge 10,000$?**

### 5.1 The Geometry of POLYDIM
- Ambient Space: $\mathbb{R}^D$ with $D \ge 10,000$.
- State Space: Unit hypersphere $\mathbb{S}^{D-1} = \{ x \in \mathbb{R}^D : \|x\|_2 = 1 \}$.
- Communication Protocol: PMTP transmits state tensors $x_k \in \mathbb{S}^{D-1}$.
- Baseline Consensus: Euclidean Projected Centroid:
  $$\bar{x}_{\text{EPC}} = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k x_k}{\left\| \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k x_k \right\|_2} \tag{30}$$
- Empirical Benchmark: EPC achieves $99.4\%$ cosine similarity under noise scaling $\sigma / \sqrt{D}$.

---

### 5.2 The Pure-State Equivalence Theorem

Let $K$ agents broadcast pure state vectors $x_1, \dots, x_K \in \mathbb{S}^{D-1}$. Represent these states as Dirac measures $\mu_k = \delta_{x_k}$.

We consider the Wasserstein barycenter problem constrained to the manifold $\mathbb{S}^{D-1}$ under two distinct ground metrics:
1. **The Ambient Chordal Metric:** $c_{\text{chord}}(x, y) = \|x - y\|_2^2$.
2. **The Intrinsic Geodesic Metric:** $c_{\text{geo}}(x, y) = d_{\mathbb{S}^{D-1}}^2(x, y) = \arccos^2(\langle x, y \rangle)$.

#### Theorem 2 (Chordal Barycenter Equivalence to EPC):
*Let $\mu_k = \delta_{x_k}$ with $x_k \in \mathbb{S}^{D-1}$ and weights $\lambda \in \Delta^K$. The Wasserstein-2 barycenter constrained to the manifold of Dirac measures on $\mathbb{S}^{D-1}$ under the chordal metric is uniquely given by the Euclidean Projected Centroid:*
$$\arg\min_{z \in \mathbb{S}^{D-1}} \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k W_2^2(\delta_z, \delta_{x_k}) \equiv \bar{x}_{\text{EPC}}$$

#### Proof:
For any candidate state $z \in \mathbb{S}^{D-1}$, the Wasserstein distance between Dirac measures is:
$$W_2^2(\delta_z, \delta_{x_k}) = \|z - x_k\|_2^2 = \|z\|_2^2 - 2 \langle z, x_k \rangle + \|x_k\|_2^2$$
Because $z \in \mathbb{S}^{D-1}$ and $x_k \in \mathbb{S}^{D-1}$, we have $\|z\|_2^2 = 1$ and $\|x_k\|_2^2 = 1$. Therefore:
$$\|z - x_k\|_2^2 = 2 - 2 \langle z, x_k \rangle$$
The barycentric objective function $J(z)$ is:
$$J(z) = \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k \left( 2 - 2 \langle z, x_k \rangle \right) = 2 - 2 \left\langle z, \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k x_k \right\rangle$$
Minimizing $J(z)$ over $z \in \mathbb{S}^{D-1}$ is equivalent to maximizing the linear functional:
$$\max_{z \in \mathbb{S}^{D-1}} \langle z, v \rangle \quad \text{where } v = \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k x_k$$
By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, for all $z \in \mathbb{S}^{D-1}$:
$$\langle z, v \rangle \le \|z\|_2 \|v\|_2 = \|v\|_2$$
with equality attained if and only if $z$ is parallel to $v$:
$$z^* = \frac{v}{\|v\|_2} = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k x_k}{\left\| \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k x_k \right\|_2} \equiv \bar{x}_{\text{EPC}}$$
$\blacksquare$

---

### 5.3 High-Dimensional Asymptotic Equivalence under Geodesic Metric

Now consider the intrinsic Riemannian Fréchet mean on $\mathbb{S}^{D-1}$:
$$\bar{x}_{\text{geo}} = \arg\min_{z \in \mathbb{S}^{D-1}} \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k \arccos^2(\langle z, x_k \rangle) \tag{31}$$

In POLYDIM, the agents communicate states that have high cosine similarity:
$$\langle x_j, x_k \rangle = 1 - \delta_{jk}, \quad \text{where } \delta_{jk} \sim \mathcal{O}\left( \frac{\sigma^2}{D} \right)$$
At $D = 10,000$ and $\sigma \sim 1$, $\delta_{jk} \approx 10^{-4} \ll 1$.

Taylor expanding $\arccos(1 - \delta)$ around $\delta = 0$:
$$\arccos(1 - \delta) = \sqrt{2\delta} \left( 1 + \frac{\delta}{12} + \frac{3\delta^2}{160} + \mathcal{O}(\delta^3) \right)$$
Squaring the arc-length:
$$\arccos^2(1 - \delta) = 2\delta \left( 1 + \frac{\delta}{6} + \mathcal{O}(\delta^2) \right) = 2\delta + \frac{\delta^2}{3} + \mathcal{O}(\delta^3)$$
Notice that the chordal distance is exactly $\|z - x_k\|_2^2 = 2\delta$. Substituting this back:
$$d_{\mathbb{S}^{D-1}}^2(z, x_k) = \|z - x_k\|_2^2 + \frac{1}{12} \|z - x_k\|_2^4 + \mathcal{O}\left( \|z - x_k\|_2^6 \right) \tag{32}$$

#### Geometric Consequence:
The intrinsic geodesic Fréchet objective differs from the chordal (EPC) objective only by higher-order terms:
$$\sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k d_{\mathbb{S}^{D-1}}^2(z, x_k) = J(z) + \frac{1}{12} \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k \|z - x_k\|_2^4 + \dots$$
Evaluating the residual gradient at $z = \bar{x}_{\text{EPC}}$:
$$\|\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{D-1}} J_{\text{geo}}(\bar{x}_{\text{EPC}})\| \le \mathcal{O}\left( \frac{\sigma^4}{D^2} \right) \approx \mathcal{O}(10^{-8})$$
At $D = 10,000$, the angular discrepancy between the true Riemannian Fréchet mean and the Euclidean Projected Centroid is bounded by:
$$\theta(\bar{x}_{\text{geo}}, \bar{x}_{\text{EPC}}) \le \mathcal{O}\left( D^{-3/2} \right) \approx 10^{-6} \text{ radians}$$
This angular difference is smaller than IEEE-754 single-precision float machine epsilon ($\epsilon_{\text{float32}} \approx 1.19 \times 10^{-7}$).

## 6. GROMOV-WASSERSTEIN FOR CROSS-DIMENSIONAL AGENT SWARMS

In heterogeneous multi-agent systems, agents may run disparate internal neural architectures where latent dimensions differ:
$$\text{Agent Swarm } \mathcal{A}: x \in \mathbb{S}^{D_1 - 1}, \quad D_1 = 10,240 \quad (\text{e.g., Einstein-10K})$$
$$\text{Agent Swarm } \mathcal{B}: y \in \mathbb{S}^{D_2 - 1}, \quad D_2 = 16,384 \quad (\text{e.g., Tensor-16K})$$

Because $D_1 \neq D_2$, the Euclidean distance $\|x - y\|$ and inner product $\langle x, y \rangle$ are mathematically undefined. Classical optimal transport cannot be formulated because no ambient metric space contains both distributions.

---

### 6.1 Mathematical Formulation of Gromov-Wasserstein (Mémoli, 2011)

The **Gromov-Wasserstein (GW)** distance generalizes optimal transport to incommensurable metric spaces by shifting the transport principle from comparing *coordinates* to comparing *intra-relational geometries*.

Let $(\mathcal{X}, d_{\mathcal{X}}, \mu)$ and $(\mathcal{Y}, d_{\mathcal{Y}}, \nu)$ be two metric measure spaces (mm-spaces), where $\mu \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})$ and $\nu \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})$.
Let $D_{\mathcal{X}}(x, x') = d_{\mathcal{X}}(x, x')$ and $D_{\mathcal{Y}}(y, y') = d_{\mathcal{Y}}(y, y')$ denote internal metric matrices or kernels.

The **Gromov-Wasserstein-$p$ distance** is defined as:
$$\operatorname{GW}_p^p(\mu, \nu) = \inf_{\gamma \in \Pi(\mu, \nu)} \int_{\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}} \int_{\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}} \left| d_{\mathcal{X}}(x, x') - d_{\mathcal{Y}}(y, y') \right|^p \, d\gamma(x, y) \, d\gamma(x', y') \tag{33}$$

#### Fundamental Invariance Properties:
1. **Metric on mm-Spaces:** $\operatorname{GW}_p(\mu, \nu) = 0$ if and only if there exists an **isometry** $\psi: \operatorname{supp}(\mu) \to \operatorname{supp}(\nu)$ such that $\psi_\sharp \mu = \nu$.
2. **Isometry Invariance:** For any orthogonal transformations $R_1 \in O(D_1)$ and $R_2 \in O(D_2)$:
   $$\operatorname{GW}_p((R_1)_\sharp \mu, \, (R_2)_\sharp \nu) = \operatorname{GW}_p(\mu, \nu) \tag{34}$$
3. **Cross-Dimensional Matching:** The formulation does not require $\mathcal{X}$ and $\mathcal{Y}$ to share the same dimension, coordinate axes, or metric space.

---

### 6.2 Entropic Gromov-Wasserstein (Peyré, Cuturi, & Solomon, 2016)

In discrete settings with source matrix $C_{\mathcal{X}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ and target matrix $C_{\mathcal{Y}} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$:
$$\operatorname{GW}_2^2(C_{\mathcal{X}}, C_{\mathcal{Y}}, a, b) = \min_{P \in U(a, b)} \mathcal{E}(P) := \sum_{i, j, k, l} \left( C_{\mathcal{X}, ik} - C_{\mathcal{Y}, jl} \right)^2 P_{ij} P_{kl} \tag{35}$$
Equation (35) is a non-convex **Quadratic Assignment Problem (QAP)**, which is NP-hard in the general case.

Peyré et al. (2016) introduced entropic regularization:
$$\operatorname{GW}_{2, \varepsilon}^2(C_{\mathcal{X}}, C_{\mathcal{Y}}, a, b) = \min_{P \in U(a, b)} \mathcal{E}(P) - \varepsilon H(P) \tag{36}$$

#### Gradient and Projected Alternating Sinkhorn:
The objective can be minimized via projected gradient descent. Expanding the quadratic cost tensor:
$$\mathcal{E}(P) = \operatorname{const} - 2 \langle C_{\mathcal{X}} P C_{\mathcal{Y}}^T, \, P \rangle_F$$
At each iteration $k$, we linearize $\mathcal{E}(P)$ around the current plan $P^{(k)}$:
$$G^{(k)} := \nabla \mathcal{E}(P^{(k)}) = C_{\mathcal{X}}^2 a \mathbf{1}_m^T + \mathbf{1}_n b^T (C_{\mathcal{Y}}^2)^T - 2 C_{\mathcal{X}} P^{(k)} C_{\mathcal{Y}}^T \tag{37}$$
The next iterate is obtained by solving a standard entropic OT problem with cost matrix $G^{(k)}$:
$$P^{(k+1)} = \arg\min_{P \in U(a, b)} \langle P, G^{(k)} \rangle_F - \varepsilon H(P) \tag{38}$$
which is solved directly using the standard Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm (Algorithm 1).

```
================================================================================
ALGORITHM 3: Entropic Gromov-Wasserstein Alignment
================================================================================
Input: Source Intra-distance C_X in R^{n x n}, Target Intra-distance C_Y in R^{m x m},
       Marginals a in Delta^n, b in Delta^m, Regularization epsilon, MaxOuterIter K_out
Output: Optimal transport alignment plan P* in U(a, b)

1: Initialize P^(0) <- a * b^T (Tensor product of marginals)
2: Compute constant terms:
     C_X2_a <- (C_X.^2) * a          // n x 1
     C_Y2_b <- (C_Y.^2) * b          // m x 1
3: for k = 0 to K_out - 1 do
4:     // Construct linearized gradient cost matrix:
       Cost_G <- C_X2_a * 1_m^T + 1_n * (C_Y2_b)^T - 2 * (C_X * P^(k) * C_Y^T)
5:     // Solve standard Sinkhorn subproblem:
       P^(k+1) <- Sinkhorn(Cost_G, a, b, epsilon)
6:     if ||P^(k+1) - P^(k)||_F < tol then
7:         break
8:     end if
9: end for
10: return P^(k+1)
================================================================================
```

### 6.3 Cross-Swarm Manifold Alignment in POLYDIM

In the POLYDIM ecosystem, Gromov-Wasserstein unlocks **zero-copy semantic alignment between heterogeneous agent swarms**:

```
+-------------------------------------------------------------+
|                     SWARM A (D_1 = 10,240)                  |
|  Agent belief states: X = [x_1, ..., x_n] in S^{D_1 - 1}   |

+-------------------------------------------------------------+
                               |
                               |  Entropic Gromov-Wasserstein
                               |  Optimization Plan P* in R^{n x m}
                               v
+-------------------------------------------------------------+

+-------------------------------------------------------------+
```

#### The Barycentric Projection Translation Operator:
Once the optimal GW alignment plan $P^* \in U(a, b)$ is obtained, an agent in Swarm $\mathcal{A}$ can project any state $x \in \mathbb{S}^{D_1 - 1}$ into Swarm $\mathcal{B}$'s latent space $\mathbb{S}^{D_2 - 1}$ without re-encoding text or collapsing to 1D token streams:
$$\hat{y}(x) = \operatorname{Proj}_{\mathbb{S}^{D_2-1}} \left( \sum_{j=1}^m \frac{P_{i(x), j}^*}{a_{i(x)}} y_j \right) \tag{39}$$
where $i(x) = \arg\min_{i} d_{\mathbb{S}^{D_1-1}}(x, x_i)$.

This preserves relational semantic topology while jumping dimensional boundaries, satisfying the zero-copy inference requirement of the POLYDIM architecture.

## 7. OPEN PROBLEMS IN HIGH-DIMENSIONAL OPTIMAL TRANSPORT

The deployment of optimal transport to ultra-high-dimensional agent communication ($D \ge 10,000$) reveals several unresolved theoretical and algorithmic challenges:

### Open Problem 1: Non-Leakage Spherical Entropic Transport
In high dimensions, entropic regularization $\varepsilon H(P)$ acts as a Gaussian smoothing prior on the ambient Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^D$. When computing Sinkhorn divergences between measures on $\mathbb{S}^{D-1}$, the unconstrained entropic displacement interpolation drifts into the interior of the unit ball $\mathbb{B}^D$ (the "hollow sphere" effect). 
- *Challenge:* Formulate an exact intrinsic spherical Gibbs kernel $K_{\mathbb{S}^{D-1}}(x, y) = \exp(-d_{\mathbb{S}^{D-1}}^2(x, y)/\varepsilon)$ whose displacement interpolation remains strictly confined to $\mathbb{S}^{D-1}$ without numerical distortion or requiring ambient projection steps.

### Open Problem 2: Sample Complexity of Manifold-Constrained Sliced OT
While the sample complexity of SWD is $\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/2})$ for measures on $\mathbb{R}^D$, tight lower and upper bounds for the intrinsic Spherical Sliced-Wasserstein (SSW) metric on $\mathbb{S}^{D-1}$ as $D \to \infty$ remain unestablished. 
- *Challenge:* Prove whether the constant pre-factors in $\mathbb{E}[|\mathcal{SSW}(\mu, \hat{\mu}_N)|] \le C(D) N^{-1/2}$ remain uniformly bounded as $D \to \infty$ under high concentration of measure ($\sigma / \sqrt{D}$ scaling).

### Open Problem 3: Streaming Decentralized Gromov-Wasserstein Consensus
The entropic GW algorithm requires computing full matrix multiplications $C_{\mathcal{X}} P C_{\mathcal{Y}}^T$ at each step, demanding $\mathcal{O}(n^2 m + n m^2)$ operations. In dynamic PMTP agent networks where packets arrive asynchronously in streaming fashion, batch GW is prohibitive.
- *Challenge:* Develop an online, streaming Gromov-Wasserstein consensus algorithm with provable regret bounds that updates alignment plans incrementally with $\mathcal{O}(D)$ per-packet complexity.

### Open Problem 4: Byzantine-Resistant Wasserstein Barycenters
In distributed agent swarms, adversarial or failing nodes broadcast corrupted states or adversarial distributions designed to pull the consensus barycenter toward destructive trajectories. 
- *Challenge:* Formulate a robust Fréchet barycenter on $\mathbb{S}^{D-1}$ incorporating trimmed Wasserstein penalties that maintains breakdown point $\alpha > 0.33$ while retaining $\mathcal{O}(K D)$ computational scalability.
